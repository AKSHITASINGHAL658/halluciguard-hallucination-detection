Solar photovoltaic power has grown faster than any other electricity source over the past decade. Global installed solar capacity passed 1,000 gigawatts in 2022 and reached roughly 1,600 gigawatts by the end of 2023, when about 345 gigawatts of new capacity were added in a single year. China accounted for more than half of those additions, installing around 217 gigawatts in 2023 alone. The United States added about 33 gigawatts in 2023, and India added roughly 7.5 gigawatts.

The cost of solar electricity has fallen dramatically. According to the International Renewable Energy Agency, the global weighted average levelized cost of electricity for utility-scale solar photovoltaic dropped from about 0.38 US dollars per kilowatt-hour in 2010 to about 0.044 dollars per kilowatt-hour in 2023, a decline of roughly 89 percent. Module prices fell below 0.15 dollars per watt in 2023, and crystalline silicon modules dominate the market with more than 95 percent share.

Commercial silicon solar cells typically reach conversion efficiencies of 20 to 23 percent. In 2023, LONGi reported a laboratory efficiency of 33.9 percent for a perovskite-silicon tandem cell, and in 2024 the company announced 34.6 percent. The theoretical Shockley-Queisser limit for a single-junction silicon cell is about 29.4 percent, which is why tandem designs attract so much research interest.

The largest solar plants are now enormous. The Bhadla Solar Park in Rajasthan, India, has a capacity of 2,245 megawatts and covers about 5,700 hectares. The Tengger Desert Solar Park in China has a capacity of about 1,547 megawatts. In the United States, the Solar Star project in California produces 579 megawatts and was commissioned in 2015.

Solar panels degrade slowly, typically losing about 0.5 percent of their output per year, and most manufacturers offer performance warranties of 25 to 30 years. Rooftop systems in Germany, Australia and the Netherlands provide a large share of household solar generation; Australia has rooftop solar on more than 3.7 million homes, roughly one in three detached houses.

Intermittency remains the main challenge. Solar output follows the sun, peaking around midday and falling to zero at night, which creates the so-called duck curve in California, where net demand drops sharply in the afternoon and then ramps up steeply in the evening. Grid operators address this with batteries, flexible demand and transmission upgrades. In 2023, solar generated about 5.5 percent of global electricity, and the International Energy Agency expects it to become the largest source of renewable electricity by the early 2030s.
