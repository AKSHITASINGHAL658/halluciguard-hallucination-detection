Geothermal energy uses heat from the Earth's interior to produce electricity and direct heating. Worldwide installed geothermal electricity capacity was about 16.9 gigawatts at the end of 2023, according to the International Renewable Energy Agency, producing roughly 97 terawatt-hours per year. That is less than 0.4 percent of global electricity, but geothermal plants run almost continuously with capacity factors often above 80 percent, which makes the energy valuable as firm, low-carbon baseload power.

The United States has the largest installed geothermal capacity at about 3.9 gigawatts, led by The Geysers complex in northern California, which has been operating since 1960 and has a net capacity of roughly 725 megawatts across 13 plants. Indonesia is second with about 2.4 gigawatts, followed by the Philippines with about 1.9 gigawatts, Turkey with about 1.7 gigawatts, and New Zealand with about 1.2 gigawatts. Kenya has around 950 megawatts, and geothermal supplies close to 45 percent of its electricity, making it one of the highest shares in the world. Iceland generates about 30 percent of its electricity from geothermal and heats roughly 90 percent of its homes with geothermal hot water.

Conventional geothermal plants need three things: heat, water and permeable rock. They are therefore limited to places such as volcanic regions and tectonic plate boundaries. Enhanced geothermal systems, or EGS, aim to remove this limitation by drilling deep wells and fracturing hot dry rock to circulate water. The Utah FORGE site, funded by the United States Department of Energy, successfully drilled a 2,600-meter deviated well in 2022. In 2023 the company Fervo Energy completed a pilot project, Project Red in Nevada, producing 3.5 megawatts, and it is building the 400-megawatt Cape Station in Utah, with first power planned for 2026.

Costs remain a barrier. The global weighted average levelized cost of geothermal electricity was about 0.071 dollars per kilowatt-hour in 2023, higher than solar and wind, mostly because drilling is expensive and exploratory wells can fail. Drilling a single deep well can cost between 5 and 10 million dollars. Technology borrowed from the oil and gas industry, such as horizontal drilling and polycrystalline diamond compact bits, is helping to reduce costs. The United States Department of Energy has set a target to cut EGS costs to 45 dollars per megawatt-hour by 2035.

Geothermal also has small land and carbon footprints. Typical lifecycle emissions are around 38 grams of carbon dioxide equivalent per kilowatt-hour, compared with about 820 grams for coal. Risks include induced seismicity, as seen at the Basel project in Switzerland, which was stopped after a magnitude 3.4 earthquake in 2006.
