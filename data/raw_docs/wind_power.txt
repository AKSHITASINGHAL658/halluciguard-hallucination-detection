Wind power is the second largest source of renewable electricity after hydropower. Global installed wind capacity reached about 1,017 gigawatts at the end of 2023, according to the Global Wind Energy Council, after a record 117 gigawatts of new installations that year. Onshore wind made up roughly 106 gigawatts of that total, while offshore wind added about 10.8 gigawatts. China again led the market with around 75 gigawatts of new capacity, followed by the United States and Brazil.

Turbines have become much larger. In 2000 a typical onshore turbine had a rated power near 1.5 megawatts and a rotor diameter of about 70 meters. Modern onshore turbines are often 4 to 6 megawatts. Offshore machines are larger still: the Vestas V236-15.0 MW has a rotor diameter of 236 meters, and the Siemens Gamesa SG 14-222 DD has a 222-meter rotor. Chinese manufacturer Mingyang has announced a 20-megawatt offshore design.

Offshore wind is concentrated in a few regions. The United Kingdom has about 14 gigawatts of offshore capacity, and the Hornsea 2 wind farm, completed in 2022, has a capacity of 1.32 gigawatts and is located 89 kilometers off the Yorkshire coast. The Dogger Bank wind farm, being built in stages, is planned to reach 3.6 gigawatts and become the largest offshore wind farm in the world. Denmark, Germany and the Netherlands also have substantial North Sea projects, while the first large United States project, Vineyard Wind 1 off Massachusetts, began delivering power in 2024 with a planned capacity of 806 megawatts.

Capacity factors differ by location. Onshore wind farms typically achieve capacity factors of 30 to 40 percent, while modern offshore farms often reach 45 to 55 percent because winds are stronger and steadier at sea. The levelized cost of onshore wind fell to about 0.033 dollars per kilowatt-hour in 2023, making it among the cheapest sources of new electricity in many countries.

Wind supplied about 7.8 percent of global electricity in 2023. In Denmark wind provided roughly 58 percent of electricity generation in 2023, the highest share among large markets, and in the United Kingdom it supplied about 29 percent. Challenges include variability, grid connection delays, supply chain bottlenecks for large blades and towers, and local opposition to onshore projects. Recycling of fiberglass blades is also an issue, as blades last about 20 to 25 years and thousands will reach end of life in the coming decade.
