Part A

Foundations: the problem and the key ideas

1. The problem: AI hallucinations

1.1 The one-minute summary

AI language models (like ChatGPT or the model used here) sometimes state things that are not true, and they say them just as confidently as things that are true. This is called a hallucination.

HalluciGuard answers questions using a small set of documents, then checks every sentence of its own answer against those documents. It colours each sentence (green = supported, amber = neutral or containing wrong parts, red = unsupported, grey = filler with no claim), marks the exact wrong words (numbers and names the sources don't contain) in bright red, shows which source words the checker focused on, and gives the whole answer a trust score between 0 and 1.

The one-paragraph explanation (memorise this)

“HalluciGuard answers questions from 6 renewable-energy documents. It finds the relevant chunks, removes useless ones (pruning), and a local AI model (qwen2.5:3b running on Ollama) writes an answer. Then a checker model (DeBERTa NLI) tests each sentence, or each smaller claim inside it, against the sources, a second check looks for numbers and names the sources do not contain, unsupported parts are marked, and a trust score is computed. We test the checker with sentences we broke on purpose and with a labelled set of real answers.”

1.2 What exactly is a hallucination?

A language model writes text by repeatedly predicting the next word. It learned which words usually follow which from huge amounts of text. It does not look facts up in a database; it produces text that sounds right. Usually that text is also correct, but sometimes it is fluent and wrong. Typical hallucinations:

  • Wrong numbers or dates: “345 GW” becomes “120 GW”, or “2023” becomes “2019”.
  • Invented details: a plausible-sounding name, statistic or quotation that appears nowhere in the sources.
  • Mixed-up facts: a fact about wind power stated as if it were about solar power.
  • Over-generalisation or commentary: “some countries” becomes “all countries”, or opinions such as “this underscores the country's commitment”.

The danger is that the user cannot tell the difference from the way the answer is written. A wrong sentence looks exactly as confident as a right one.

1.3 Why it matters

If people use AI answers for studying, business, medicine or law, a confident wrong answer can cause real harm. Organisations that use AI on their own documents need to know which parts of an answer actually come from their documents and which parts the AI added on its own. HalluciGuard addresses exactly this need.

2. The big idea: an open-book exam with a teacher

  2. They look up the relevant pages in the book.

3. The most important idea: “supported” is not the same as “true”

HalluciGuard checks whether a sentence is supported by the documents. It does not check whether the sentence is true in the real world. Professors very often ask about this.

QuestionCan we check it?
True (factual)Is it correct in the real world?Very hard: you would need all the world's knowledge, always up to date
Supported (grounded / faithful)Do our documents back it up?Yes: this is what HalluciGuard does
Example

Our documents say nothing about the capital of Australia. If the AI writes “Australia has Canberra as its capital”, the sentence is true, but the documents don't say it, so it is marked unsupported. (This exact sentence appeared in our test data.) The opposite can also happen: if a document contained a mistake and the AI copied it, the sentence would be marked supported even though it is false.

So in this project, red does not mean “false”. It means: “this cannot be verified from the sources, so don't trust it blindly.” That includes true facts from the model's own knowledge and unsupported commentary such as “this achievement underscores the country's commitment to renewable energy”.

Key idea

In this project: Hallucination = a claim that is not supported by the source documents. Researchers call this property faithfulness or groundedness.

What to say

Question: “Your system marked a true sentence red. Isn't that a mistake?”
Answer: “No. We detect unsupported claims, not false ones. The sentence may be true, but the sources don't back it, so the user can't verify it.”

4. Key AI concepts you need

Each concept is explained without maths first; where a formula helps, it is explained in words.

4.1 Tokens

AI models do not read whole words; they read tokens, which are words or pieces of words. “solar” might be one token, while “gigawatts” might be split into “giga” + “watts”. A rough rule: 100 tokens ≈ 75 English words. Models can only read a limited number of tokens at once. The checker model (DeBERTa) reads at most 512 tokens (the code truncates the chunk, never the sentence). This limit shapes the chunk size.

4.2 Embeddings: turning meaning into numbers

An embedding is a list of numbers that represents the meaning of a piece of text. HalluciGuard uses a small model called MiniLM (all-MiniLM-L6-v2), which turns any text into a list of 384 numbers. Texts with similar meaning get similar numbers: picture each text as a point on a map, where solar texts cluster together, wind texts cluster elsewhere, and so on.

Figure 1. Illustration of embeddings as a meaning map (real embeddings have 384 dimensions, not 2). Dashed lines = the 6 nearest chunks.

Cosine similarity measures how close two embeddings are: think of each embedding as an arrow, and ask how much two arrows point the same way (1 = same direction, same meaning).

4.3 Probabilities, logprobs and softmax

  • When the generator writes a word it has a probability for every possible next word. The probability of the word it chose tells us how confident it was. A logprob is the logarithm of that probability: 0 means certain, more negative means less confident.
  • The checker outputs three probabilities (entailment, neutral, contradiction) that add up to 1.

Models first produce raw scores; softmax turns them into probabilities that are positive and add up to 1.

4.4 Transformers and attention

MiniLM and DeBERTa are transformer models, the same family as GPT. The key mechanism is attention: when the model processes a word, it decides how much every other word in the input matters for understanding it.

  • A transformer has stacked layers (DeBERTa-base has 12), each with several attention heads (12). Different heads can focus on different relationships, e.g. matching numbers.
  • Self-attention means the words of one input attend to each other. DeBERTa receives the chunk and the answer sentence joined into one input, so words in the sentence can attend to words in the chunk.

4.5 Classifiers vs generators

KindWhat it doesIn HalluciGuard
GeneratorWrites new text, word by wordqwen2.5:3b (writes the answer; also writes one kind of test attack)
ClassifierReads text and picks a labelDeBERTa (entailment / neutral / contradiction)

Using a classifier as the checker, instead of another generator, is deliberate: classifiers are fast, give the same answer every time, cannot invent text, and give probabilities we can put a threshold on.

Check yourself

Why does the system turn text into embeddings before it can find relevant chunks?
Answer: computers compare numbers precisely. Embeddings let the system measure “closeness of meaning” as a number, so it finds chunks that mean the same thing as the question even when the words differ.

6. Step 0: Preparing the documents (ingestion)

Before anyone can ask a question, the documents are prepared once by running scripts/seed_corpus.py.

6.1 The corpus

The corpus is 6 documents (about 400 words each) on renewable energy: solar, wind, hydropower, geothermal, energy storage, and policy and investment. They contain specific numbers and dates, which gives the checker something concrete to verify and the adversarial test something to corrupt. They were written for the project, which keeps the experiment controlled but means the results are not a public benchmark.

6.2 Chunking

Each document is cut into word-based windows of 250 words that advance 210 words at a time, so neighbouring chunks overlap by 40 words. The cutting is not sentence-aware (it counts words, and rejoins them with single spaces). The 6 documents produce 12 chunks (2 per document).

  • Precision: finding one fact in one page is easier than in a whole book.
  • The checker's reading limit: DeBERTa reads at most 512 tokens, chunk and sentence together; about 250 words of chunk leaves room for the sentence.
  • Shorter prompts: the generator only gets the few chunks that matter.
  • Why overlap? A fact at the boundary of two chunks could be cut in half; with 40 words of overlap it appears complete in at least one.
Be careful

Chunk size is a trade-off. Smaller chunks suit the checker better but can separate a sentence from the context it depends on (for example a pronoun from the name it refers to). 250 words is a reasonable middle; testing other sizes is future work.

6.3 Embedding and storing

Each chunk is embedded by MiniLM and stored in ChromaDB (cosine space); the chunk text, position and document details go to SQLite. Each document has an authority score between 0 and 1, used in the trust score. In the current corpus all six documents have the same authority, 0.8 (the default), so this component carries no information yet (see section 13.3).

7. Step 1: Retrieval — finding the right chunks

The question is turned into an embedding with the same MiniLM model. ChromaDB then returns the 6 chunks closest to the question (cosine similarity). These are the retrieved chunks.

7.1 Why embeddings instead of word search?

Meaning matters more than exact words. If the user asks “How much solar power was installed?” and a document says “new photovoltaic capacity added”, a word search finds almost nothing in common, but the embeddings are close.

7.2 The weakness of retrieval

Embeddings are deliberately “loose”. A chunk about “wind capacity added globally in 2023” is also close to our solar question. So the top 6 always contain related but useless chunks, and irrelevant context can confuse the generator. That is why the next step exists.

Check yourself

Why is a wind chunk a problem when the question is about solar?
Answer: the generator might mix wind facts into the solar answer, a hallucination caused by bad context.

8. Step 2: MI pruning — removing useless chunks

Pruning looks at the 6 retrieved chunks and keeps only those that genuinely match the question. 6 chunks usually become about 2.

8.1 The question pruning asks

Key idea

“Does this chunk use the question's words more than the other retrieved chunks do?”

A word that appears everywhere (“energy”) says nothing about which chunk is relevant; a word concentrated in one chunk (“solar” in a solar chunk) is a strong signal. This is measured with PMI.

8.2 PMI in plain words

PMI (pointwise mutual information) compares, for each word of the question, how often it appears in this chunk with how often it appears across all the retrieved chunks:

PMI(question, chunk) = average over question words t of
    ln( (P(t | chunk) + 0.000001) / (P(t | all retrieved chunks) + 0.000001) )
(counts are smoothed by adding 1 to every word count)

Question words are lower-cased, stop words (“the”, “was”...) are removed and repeated words counted once. Equal frequency scores 0, more common in the chunk is positive, less common is negative.

Example

Question words: “solar”, “capacity”, “added”, “2023”. If “solar” is 6 times more common in Chunk A than on average, it adds ln 6 ≈ 1.8 to Chunk A's average. In a wind chunk “solar” never appears, giving a negative contribution. Words like “capacity” appear everywhere and contribute about 0.

8.3 The pruning rules

  1. Relevance cut-off: go through the chunks from best to worst score and keep a chunk only if its score is at least half of the best score. (If every score is zero or negative, the cut-off is relaxed to twice the best score.)
  2. Duplicate removal: also skip a chunk if its embedding similarity to a chunk already kept is above 0.85.
  3. Safety net: always keep at least 2 chunks; if the rules left fewer, top up with the next-best chunks, preferring non-duplicates.
Figure 3. Illustration of pruning six retrieved chunks.

8.4 Why prune?

  • Fewer context-induced hallucinations: the generator is not distracted by off-topic chunks.
  • Shorter prompts: less to process.
  • Cleaner verification: fewer chunks to compare each sentence against.

8.5 Why PMI and not a trained model?

Mutual information can also be estimated with neural networks, but those need training data and time. The PMI formula is closed-form, fast, and every score can be explained.

8.6 The result

Pruning drops about 62% of retrieved chunks (6 → about 2) and precision rises from 0.33 to 0.74. The ablation (section 18.3) also shows that pruning improves the checker itself: feeding all 6 chunks to NLI lowered recall from 0.84 to 0.67 and F1 from 0.71 to 0.65.

Check yourself

A wind chunk mentions “2023”, which is in the question. Why is it still dropped?
Answer: “2023” appears in most retrieved chunks, so it adds little; and the chunk never mentions “solar”, which pulls its score below half of the best.

9. Step 3: Generation — the AI writes the answer

9.1 What the generator receives

The kept chunks are numbered and placed in a prompt with the question. A system instruction tells the model how to answer. The model runs at temperature 0 (always the most likely next word), so the same prompt gives the same answer.

ModeSystem instruction (verbatim)
Strict (default)“Answer the question using only the numbered context passages below. Respond in 3-6 short sentences. If the context does not contain the answer, say so.”
Loose (test mode)“Answer the question thoroughly in 4-6 sentences. The context passages below are a starting point; enrich the answer with additional background, specific figures, dates, named examples and comparisons from your own knowledge. Do not mention the context or say when information is missing.”
Example answer

“In 2023, about 345 gigawatts of new solar capacity was added globally. This information is directly stated in the context provided.”
(Second sentence is filler: it makes no claim, see section 10.7.)

Be careful

The model is told to use only the context, but it doesn't always obey. This is exactly why verification is needed.

9.2 Logprobs: the model's confidence

The generator returns a logprob for every word it writes. Averaging them (and converting back with exp) gives the model's typical per-word probability, which becomes the token-probability part of the trust score. If a model returned no logprobs, this component is simply missing and the other weights are scaled up (section 13.6).

9.3 The cache

Every answer is saved on disk, keyed by the model, the system instruction and the exact prompt. If the same prompt is sent again, the saved answer is reused. This makes re-running evaluations fast. (The prompt is part of the key, so strict and loose answers never mix.)

9.4 Why a local model (Ollama) and not an online API?

The project first used Google's Gemini API, then removed it completely. Two problems appeared: the Gemini models available rejected logprobs (“Logprobs is not enabled”), and the free tier allowed only a small number of requests per day, which made repeated evaluation impossible. With Ollama running qwen2.5:3b (a 3-billion-parameter model):

  • there are no usage limits and nothing leaves the computer;
  • per-token logprobs are returned, so all four trust components work;
  • generation takes about 2.5 seconds; a whole /query (retrieval, generation, NLI) takes about 8 seconds, of which NLI is about 3.
  • the API preloads the models at startup and asks Ollama to keep the model in memory (30 minutes), so even the first query is fast.
Key idea

A side effect worth knowing: small local models hallucinate somewhat more than large online ones. For a project about detecting hallucinations that is useful, because the checker has real errors to catch. In strict mode though, this model is mostly faithful (section 18), which is why the test modes exist.

10. Step 4: NLI verification — checking each sentence

This is the heart of the project. The answer is split into sentences (NLTK), compound sentences are split into smaller claims, and each claim is checked against each kept chunk using NLI.

10.1 What NLI is

NLI (natural language inference) answers one question about two texts: “If Text A is true, is Text B also true?” Premise (Text A) = a chunk from the documents. Hypothesis (Text B) = the claim being checked.

LabelMeaningExample (premise: “In 2023 the world added about 345 GW of new solar capacity.”)
EntailmentThe premise supports the hypothesis“More than 300 GW of solar was added in 2023.”
NeutralThe premise doesn't say either way“Solar panels are popular in India.”
ContradictionThe premise says the opposite“Only 100 GW of solar was added in 2023.”

“More than 300” is not written in the premise, but 345 is more than 300, so it is supported. NLI judges meaning, not copied words.

10.2 Why the chunk is the premise and the sentence is the hypothesis

We want to know whether the sources support the answer, so the source plays “what is known to be true” and the answer is the claim under test. Swapping them would ask whether the answer supports the whole chunk, which it almost never does.

10.3 The model: DeBERTa

The checker is DeBERTa-v3-base, specifically MoritzLaurer/DeBERTa-v3-base-mnli-fever-anli, a Microsoft transformer further trained for NLI on MNLI (many kinds of text), FEVER (claims checked against Wikipedia) and ANLI (hard pairs written to fool models). It learned patterns such as “not” flipping meaning, or different numbers for the same thing signalling contradiction. Nobody wrote these as rules. The model is used pretrained, not fine-tuned (section 20).

10.6 Atomic claims: splitting compound sentences

Many answer sentences contain two facts joined by “and”. If one is wrong and one is right, a whole-sentence check can be fooled. So compound sentences are split, using spaCy's grammar analysis, at coordinated verbs. A clause that has no subject, or only a pronoun like “it”, borrows the main clause's subject so it can stand alone:

Example

“The Three Gorges Dam has a capacity of 22,500 megawatts and it typically generates between 85 and 100 terawatt-hours per year.”
→ claim 1: “The Three Gorges Dam has a capacity of 22,500 megawatts.”
→ claim 2: “The Three Gorges Dam typically generates between 85 and 100 terawatt-hours per year.”

The split only happens when it is safe: at most 4 claims, each at least 4 words, otherwise the sentence stays whole. A sentence takes the verdict of its weakest claim (the lowest best-entailment); it is flagged if any claim is flagged. This does not fix pronouns that refer to a previous sentence (“It was completed in 2012.”).

Be careful

In the ablation (section 18.3) claim splitting did not measurably improve F1 (0.73 vs 0.74 for sentence-level NLI; the intervals overlap). Present it as a reasonable design that was measured and gave no clear gain.

10.7 Filler sentences

Some sentences make no claim: “This information is directly stated in the context provided.” or “The context does not contain the answer.” A sentence is filler when, after removing [1]-style citations, it has no digit, is at most 25 words, and mentions a reference word (context, passage, source, document...) together with a meta word (stated, provided, mentioned, according, found, does not contain...). Filler is shown in grey italics, is never flagged, and is excluded from the sentence score, the document score and the evaluation counts. A claim sentence is any sentence that is not filler.

10.8 The decision rule (exactly how a sentence gets its colour)

The ablation sweep (section 18.3) found that thresholds from 0.3 to 0.9 all give F1 of about 0.71-0.72, so the result is not sensitive to it.

10.9 Why sentence by sentence?

If the whole answer were checked at once, five correct sentences and one wrong one would still mostly “agree” with the sources and the error would hide. Checking each sentence separately finds exactly which claim is unsupported (sentence-level hallucination localisation).

10.10 Similar words are not the same meaning

A classic trap

Premise: “Wind capacity grew in 2023.” Hypothesis: “Solar capacity grew in 2023.” Five of six words match, but the premise says nothing about solar. The correct label is neutral. A word-matching checker would wrongly say “supported”; this is why NLI is used.

11. Step 5: The number and name checker (span check)

Testing showed that NLI is weak with exact numbers: a sentence with a wrong figure once got entailment 0.97. Wrong numbers are among the most common and most harmful hallucinations, so a second, simpler check was added. It also lets the dashboard mark exactly which words are wrong instead of colouring the whole sentence.

11.1 What it checks

  • Numbers (including years): every standalone number in the sentence must appear among the numbers of the source passage the sentence is about. A number that does not is marked bright red.
  • Names: every person, organisation, place, nationality, facility, product, event or law name (found by spaCy) must appear somewhere in any kept chunk. A name that does not is marked bright red.

11.2 Exactly how matching works

StepRule
Which number countsStandalone numbers like 345, 22,500, 7.5, 2023. Skipped: numbers inside words (COP28), citations [1], and numbers followed by letters (2030s, 5th); number words (“three”)

A sentence with any wrong part becomes flagged, is shown amber with the wrong words in bright red, and its displayed source becomes the reference chunk.

Example (faulty mode)

Original: “In 2023, about 345 gigawatts of new solar capacity was added globally.”
Corrupted: “In 2013, about 690 gigawatts of new solar capacity was added globally.”
NLI said entailment (0.80), but neither 2013 nor 690 is in the Solar chunk, so both are marked.

The check confirms that a number exists in the source passage, not that it is attached to the right fact. The two checks cover each other:

What to say

“NLI judges the meaning of the sentence; the number checker makes sure the specific numbers and names exist in the sources. Each covers the other's weakness, and the second one tells the user exactly which words to distrust.”

12. Step 6: Attention attribution — where was the checker looking?

For sentences that NLI itself rejected (best entailment below 0.5), the system shows the top 5 words in the best source chunk that the checker paid most attention to. (Sentences flagged only by the number check do not get this view, because NLI accepted them.)

Figure 5. Illustrative attention view: darker source words received more attention from the flagged sentence.

12.1 How it is computed

  1. Run DeBERTa on (best chunk + flagged sentence) and ask it to also return its attention weights.
  2. Average the weights over the last 4 layers and all 12 heads.
  3. Keep only attention from the sentence's tokens to the chunk's tokens, and add it up for each chunk token.
  4. Merge word pieces back into whole words (“giga” + “watts” → “gigawatts”).
  5. Remove special tokens, punctuation and common words (“the”, “of”).
  6. Return the top 5 words, with weights scaled to add up to 1 over those five.

12.2 The correct way to describe it

Be careful

Attention shows where the checker focused. It does not prove what caused its decision; research shows attention weights and the true reasons don't always match. Say “the source words the checker attended to most”. DeBERTa uses self-attention over the joined pair, not cross-attention.

12.3 The masking check: do those words really matter?

To test the attention words instead of just trusting them, the system runs a mask-and-rerun check on every NLI-rejected sentence: it deletes the top 5 words from the source chunk, runs NLI again, and measures how far the three label probabilities move (total variation distance, 0 = no change, 1 = completely different). As a control, it does the same after deleting 5 random content words (averaged over 5 draws). The attention words are supported when their shift beats the random shift by at least 0.05. The dashboard shows this line in the sentence's expander.

Example

Sentence: “The dam has an installed capacity of 5,000 megawatts.” Top attention words include 22,500, the true figure in the source. Deleting them moves the probabilities by 0.85; deleting random words moves them by 0.01: the attention words clearly mattered.

Result on the labelled set

For the 69 sentences NLI flagged, deleting the top words moved the probabilities by 0.24 on average versus 0.11 for random words, but the top words beat the random control in only 35% of sentences (24 of 69). So attention words are a useful hint on average, not a reliable per-sentence explanation. Say this plainly.

13. Step 7: The hierarchical trust score

The trust score summarises how reliable an answer is as one number from 0 to 1. It combines four signals:

ComponentWeightQuestion it answersLevel
Sentence0.35Do the sources support the claims? (from NLI and the wrong-part check)Sentence
Entity0.25Do the names, numbers and dates in the answer appear in the sources?Entity
Document0.20How trustworthy are the documents behind the supported sentences?Document
Token probability0.20How confident was the generator while writing?Token
trust = 0.35 x sentence + 0.25 x entity + 0.20 x document + 0.20 x token probability

13.1 Sentence score

Over the claim sentences (filler excluded): the average best-entailment probability × (1 − fraction of sentences flagged). A flagged sentence lowers both factors. If there are no claim sentences the score is 0.

13.2 Entity score

spaCy finds every entity in the whole answer (names, numbers, dates, percentages...). Relative dates (“the end of that year”) are skipped, hedge words (“approximately”, “roughly”, “the”) are removed, duplicates counted once. An entity is supported if its lower-cased text appears (as a substring) in the kept chunks. The score is the fraction supported; no entities means 1.0.

13.3 Document score

The average authority of the documents behind the non-flagged claim sentences; 0 if none. All six documents currently have authority 0.8, so this component only varies with how many sentences are supported, and carries no extra information about source quality (the AUROC is 0.46, section 18.3).

13.4 Token-probability score

exp(average logprob) over the generator's words: its typical per-word probability. It describes the original answer, so in faulty mode it is dropped.

13.5 A worked example

Figure 6. Component scores for an example answer. Weighted sum: 0.35x0.90 + 0.25x1.00 + 0.20x0.80 + 0.20x0.94 = 0.913.

13.6 When a component is missing: renormalisation

If a component has no data, its weight is removed and the others are scaled up to add to 1. Without token probability the other weights add to 0.80, so they become 0.35/0.80 = 0.4375, 0.25/0.80 = 0.3125 and 0.20/0.80 = 0.25.

Figure 7. Renormalisation keeps the total weight at 1.
Example (faulty mode, no token probability)

A corrupted answer had sentence 0.76, entity 0.50 and document 0.80: 0.4375×0.76 + 0.3125×0.50 + 0.25×0.80 = 0.333 + 0.156 + 0.200 ≈ 0.69.

13.7 Why “hierarchical”, and why combine four signals?

Each signal works at a different level: tokens (words), entities (specific facts), sentences (whole claims) and documents (source quality). Each alone has blind spots: NLI alone gave a wrong number 0.97 entailment; entity matching alone cannot tell whether a number is attached to the right fact; document authority says nothing about whether a claim matches.

Key idea
Confidence is not correctness, but it is informative. On our labelled answers the generator's token probability separated good from bad answers best (AUROC 0.98, section 18.3). Caveat: loose-prompt answers are both less confident and more often unsupported, so part of that signal is the prompt mode.

13.8 How the weights were chosen

The weights were set by design, not learned. Sentence support gets the most weight as the most direct evidence. The ablation shows token probability would deserve more weight than 0.20 on this data, and the document component deserves less; learning the weights from labelled data is future work.

What to say

“The weights are designed, not learned. Our own analysis suggests token probability is stronger than its weight implies, and the document component is uninformative while all authorities are equal. I would learn the weights from more labelled data.”

13.9 Known edge cases

  • One claim, flagged: the sentence and document scores become 0 and the trust score drops to about 0 (we saw 0.00 for a faulty-mode answer with one claim). A smoother formula would help.
  • Only filler: sentence and document scores are 0, so trust is low.

Testing the system and understanding the results

15. The adversarial test: does the checker actually work?

To know whether a checker is good, you must give it sentences you already know are wrong and see whether it catches them. That is what the adversarial test does (“adversarial” = deliberately trying to break the system).

15.1 Normal use vs testing

Normal use (every question)Adversarial test (separate script)
InputThe generator's real answerSentences we broke on purpose
Do we know the right answer?No: that is what we're trying to find outYes: we know exactly which sentences are wrong
PurposeFind hallucinations in answersMeasure how good the checker is
Key idea

In normal use, nothing fake is added. The adversarial test is a separate measurement of the checker itself, like giving a teacher an answer sheet with known mistakes to see how many they find.

15.2 How it works

Figure 8. The adversarial test design.
  1. Which sentences: chunks are shuffled with a fixed seed (42) and up to 20 are taken; the corpus has only 12, so all 12 are used. From each chunk up to 2 sentences are picked (sentences with a number first), each at least 5 words and containing a number or a verb: 24 sentences.
  2. Corrupt it with each attack below; the unchanged sentence is the control.
  3. Run NLI with the original clean chunk as premise and the corrupted sentence as hypothesis.
  4. Detected means NLI's top label is anything other than entailment. Each run replaces the previous results: 24 sentences × 5 variants = 120 tests.

15.3 The attack types

AttackExact ruleExample
NegationIf “not” or “never” is present, remove the first; otherwise insert “not” after the first is/was/are/were/has/have/had/can/will/does/did; otherwise prefix “It is not true that”“Solar added 345 GW.” → “Solar did not add 345 GW.”
Stale factEvery year (1500-2099) shifts by the same random 3 to 10 years (earlier or later); every other number is multiplied by a random choice of 0.5, 0.6, 0.75, 1.5 or 2“...in 2023” → “...in 2017”; “345 GW” → “172 GW”
NoiseHalf the time (if the sentence has a name and another is available) swap one name for a random name from the corpus; otherwise append one of four unrelated clauses“...in China” → “...in Brazil”
LLM contradictionAsk qwen2.5:3b (temperature 0): “Rewrite this sentence so it remains fluent and plausible but states something factually different. Output only the sentence.”; results are cachedA fluent sentence with one detail changed

The first three are rule-based; the fourth uses the generator for subtler attacks. The “automated adversarial agent” in the proposal is this script: a program with a policy for choosing sentences and attacks, not a trained reinforcement-learning agent.

15.4 The control set: why it is essential

The unchanged true sentences are also checked; they should pass. When the checker flags an unchanged sentence, that is a false positive.

Key idea

Without a control set, a checker that flags everything would score 100% detection and look perfect. The control set shows whether the checker is accurate or just suspicious of everything.

15.5 Why not corrupt the documents and ask normal questions?

That measures nothing: the generator would faithfully repeat the corrupted text, and NLI would correctly say the (corrupted) source supports it. Comparing corrupted sentences against the clean source is what makes the test meaningful.

15.6 Interpreting the results

Every corrupted sentence is scored twice: with NLI alone (the base checker) and with the full pipeline (claim splitting, the number and name check and filler logic, using the clean chunk as the only evidence). Both give 100% on negation, changed numbers and noise. On LLM-written contradictions NLI alone detects 21% and the full pipeline 29%; the false-positive rate on unchanged sentences is 16.7% for NLI alone and 20.8% for the full pipeline: the extra checks catch a little more and flag a little more.

LLM-written contradictions are detected only 21-29% of the time. Two explanations, and both may be true:

  • The attacks are weak: the small 3B model often wrote near-paraphrases (“approximately”, a small year change) instead of real contradictions; a “contradiction” that does not contradict is correctly passed by the checker.
  • The checker is weak on subtle changes: fluent rewrites with a small shift in meaning are genuinely harder for NLI than obvious negations.
Be careful

Until someone reads the attack sentences and marks which are real contradictions, you cannot say how much of the 21% is due to each cause. Say: “Detection on LLM-written attacks was 21%; many rewrites were near-paraphrases, so this test is weak.”

16. Test modes: faulty mode and loose mode

The checker's value is only visible when an answer contains errors. Two clearly labelled test modes create errors on purpose:

Why test modes are legitimate

This is not cheating. The project's purpose is detecting hallucinations, and a detector can only be tested on answers that contain some. The modes are labelled openly, and normal mode stays strict, as real RAG systems are. In the report: “We used a loose prompt to create more hallucinations for testing.”

In loose mode many red sentences may be true facts from the model's general knowledge, or plain commentary. By the project's definition (section 3) they are still unsupported, so flagging them is correct.

17.3 Why precision@6 can never exceed 0.33 here

Each document produces only 2 chunks. If the answer is in one document, at most 2 of the 6 retrieved chunks can come from it: the best possible precision is 2/6 = 0.33. The measured 0.33 is a ceiling of the setup, not a failure of retrieval. Recall@k and hit rate would suit this setup better.

Professors often ask “why did you choose X instead of Y?”. Each decision has a short, honest reason.

21. Future work

  • Sentence-level evidence: check each claim against the 2-3 most relevant source sentences instead of a whole 250-word chunk (likely the biggest single improvement for precision and for explanation).
  • Human-verified labels: have a person re-label at least a sample of the labelled set, and extend it.
  • Learn the trust weights from labelled answers; give documents real authority values; smooth the single-claim case.
  • A second verifier or numeric reasoning (units, magnitude, rounding) to raise recall on contradictions (0.44).
  • Pronoun retry: re-check flagged pronoun sentences with the previous sentence attached.
  • Citations: make the generator cite which chunk supports each sentence, then verify the citation.
  • Manual review of LLM attacks and a larger attack set.
  • Retrieval metrics that suit 2-chunk documents (recall@k, hit rate); a chunk-size experiment; a larger corpus and more questions.
  • Better attribution: the masking check shows attention words are not reliable per sentence; try other attribution methods; a larger NLI model (more accurate, slower).
  • Done already (no longer future work): threshold sweep, labelled-set precision/recall/F1 (AI-labelled), ablation of each component, the attention masking check, and scoring the attacks through the full pipeline.

22. Question bank: likely questions and answers

Every answer follows the same pattern: what we did, why, and its limit.

22.1 The project as a whole

Q: What problem does your project solve?

AI models can state unsupported things confidently. HalluciGuard checks each sentence of a RAG answer against the sources, marks the unsupported parts, and gives a trust score.

Q: What is new compared to a normal RAG system?

Normal RAG stops after generating. We add sentence- and claim-level verification with NLI, a number and name checker that marks the exact wrong words, attention-based explanations, a hierarchical trust score, MI pruning, and an evaluation of the checker (adversarial test, labelled set and ablation).

Q: Do you detect false information?

No: we detect claims not supported by the sources. A true fact not in the documents is still unverified, and a wrong fact copied from a wrong document is marked supported.

Q: Did you train or fine-tune any model?

No. All models are used pretrained: DeBERTa NLI, MiniLM embeddings, spaCy and the qwen2.5:3b generator. The weights of the trust score and the thresholds are set by design.

22.2 Retrieval and pruning

Q: Why split documents into chunks?

Smaller pieces are easier to search precisely, and the checker can only read about 512 tokens at once.

Q: Why embeddings and not keyword search?

Embeddings match meaning, so different words with the same meaning are still found.

Q: Why is precision only 0.33?

Each document has about 2 chunks, so at most 2 of 6 retrieved chunks can be relevant: a ceiling of the setup. After pruning, precision is 0.74.

Q: What does PMI measure, and does pruning really help?

Whether the question's words are more common in a chunk than across all retrieved chunks. Yes: in the ablation, feeding NLI all 6 chunks lowered recall from 0.84 to 0.67 and F1 from 0.71 to 0.65.

Q: Why keep at least 2 chunks?

A safety net so pruning never leaves the generator without enough evidence.

22.3 Verification

Q: Why NLI and not an LLM judge?

It is local, fast, gives the same result every time, and outputs probabilities we can threshold. An LLM judge is slower and can hallucinate too.

Q: Why is the chunk the premise?

We are asking whether the source supports the answer, so the source is what we treat as known.

Q: Why 0.5? Did you tune it?

It is the midpoint where support becomes more likely than not, a design choice. We swept it from 0.3 to 0.9 on the labelled set: F1 stayed at 0.71-0.72, so the result is not sensitive to it; higher thresholds trade precision for recall.

Q: Why sentence by sentence, and what are claims?

Checking the whole answer hides a single wrong sentence. Compound sentences are further split into claims so one wrong fact is not hidden by a right one; the sentence takes its weakest claim's verdict.

Q: Your false-positive rate is 16.7%. Isn't that bad?

It is a main weakness (23% of supported sentences in the labelled set). Known causes: pronouns, long chunks, number formats. Smaller evidence snippets and a pronoun retry are the next fixes.

Q: Why a number checker? And did it help?

NLI is weak with exact numbers (a wrong figure once got 0.97 entailment). The checker confirms numbers and names exist in the source and marks the exact words. On our labelled set, which is mostly invented details and commentary, it did not raise overall F1, but it catches changed numbers and makes the explanation precise.

Q: Couldn't a number match in the wrong context?

Yes. We choose the passage the sentence is about by meaning, not by NLI, to reduce this, and NLI still judges the sentence; but a number that exists in the right passage attached to a different fact would pass.

Q: Why not fine-tune DeBERTa?

It needs a large labelled dataset and a GPU, and a strict split between training and test data; the proposal specified a pretrained model.

22.4 Attention and trust

Q: What does the attention view show?

The source words the checker focused on most for a sentence NLI rejected. It is an explanation aid, not proof of what caused the decision.

Q: How do you know the attention words are meaningful?

We test them: delete the top 5 words from the source, re-run NLI, and compare the change with deleting 5 random words. On average the top words move the probabilities more (0.24 vs 0.11), but they beat the random control in only 35% of flagged sentences, so we present them as a hint, not as proof.

Q: Is this cross-attention?

No: DeBERTa uses self-attention over the joined chunk and sentence. We read the part from sentence tokens to chunk tokens.

Q: Why these trust weights?

Sentence support is the most direct evidence, so it has the largest weight. They are designed, not learned. Our analysis suggests token probability is stronger than its 0.20 weight implies and the document component is uninformative while all authorities are equal; learning the weights is future work.

Q: Why combine four signals?

Each has blind spots. NLI missed a wrong number that the entity check caught; the number check cannot judge meaning; model confidence alone does not show which sentence is wrong.

Q: What if a model gives no logprobs?

The token component's weight is dropped and the other weights are scaled to sum to 1.

22.5 Testing

Q: How do you know the checker works?

Three ways: (1) we corrupt true sentences and check detection (100% for negation, changed numbers and noise; 21% for weak LLM rewrites) with a control set for false positives; (2) a labelled set of 176 real answer sentences with precision, recall and F1; (3) an ablation of each component.

Q: Who labelled the data?

An AI assistant, reading each sentence against the source documents; it was not human-verified. We state that openly; a human re-label of a sample is the next step.

Q: Why compare against the clean chunk?

If we corrupted the documents, the generator would repeat the corruption and NLI would correctly call it supported, so nothing would be measured.

Q: Why a control set?

Without it, a checker that flags everything would look perfect.

Q: Why only 21% on LLM attacks?

The 3B model often wrote near-paraphrases rather than real contradictions, so the test is weak; subtle rewrites are also genuinely harder for NLI. The full pipeline improves it only a little, to 29%.

Q: Isn't faulty or loose mode cheating?

No: a detector can only be shown on answers with errors. The modes are labelled, and normal mode stays strict.

Q: Why Ollama instead of Gemini?

The Gemini models we could use refused logprobs and had a small daily limit. Ollama has no limits, returns logprobs, and is private and reproducible. Gemini was removed from the project.

Q: How big is your evaluation?

22 questions, 6 documents and 176 labelled sentences: indicative, not a benchmark.

24. Running the demo

24.1 Before presenting

  1. Start Ollama: ollama serve (and once: ollama pull qwen2.5:3b).
  2. Start the backend: .venv/bin/python -m uvicorn app.main:app --port 8000. It preloads the models at startup (about 12 seconds).
  3. Start the dashboard: .venv/bin/python -m streamlit run dashboard/streamlit_app.py and open http://localhost:8501.
  4. Using the .venv/bin/python -m ... form avoids starting a global Python that lacks the packages.
  5. Run one test question so everything is warm.
  6. Test your questions the day before, and keep screenshots or a screen recording as a backup.
  7. For a loose-mode demo, start the backend with PROMPT_MODE=loose and restart it without afterwards.

24.2 Demo script (about 3 minutes)

  1. Ask a normal question. Show the green sentences, the grey filler sentence, the entity list and the trust score. Point out “6 retrieved → 2 used”.
  2. Tick Faulty mode and ask again. Show the bright red wrong numbers inside the amber sentence, the struck-through original, the “Caught” line, the lower trust score, and the source passage in the expander.
  3. Open the Robustness tab: detection per attack type and the false-positive rate.
  4. Open the Evaluation tab for the headline numbers (this calls the generator for all 22 questions, so run it beforehand or rely on README.md).
  5. Mention the ablation table (section 18.3) and be ready to say the labels were made by an AI assistant.
Tip

Good demo questions: one the documents answer fully (all green); one they answer only partly; and one completely off-topic (“Who won the 2022 football World Cup?”), where the model should say the context has no answer and the sentence appears as grey filler.

Be careful

In faulty mode a one-claim answer can show a trust score of 0.00. That is the formula working as designed (section 13.9), but be ready to explain it.
